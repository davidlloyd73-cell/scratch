Road to 2028

A Monte Carlo of the 2028 Electoral College, seeded with the 2026 midterm result and run for 21 head-to-head matchups. The midterm swing is large, but the history of out-party midterm waves says only part of it survives into a presidential year. How much survives, how Latino voters behave, and what happens with Iran and the economy are the dials below.

How the model works. The 2026 swing is split into a Latino part and a non-Latino part, and each keeps a chosen fraction (retention) into 2028. History points to roughly half: the 2018 House margin of D+8.6 became D+4.5 in 2020, and 2022's R+2.8 became R+1.5 in 2024. On top of that: a national drift (SD 4.5 points, reflecting two-year-ahead forecast error), an Iran outcome (−1.0 for Democrats if the war ends durably, +1.5 if it does not), a recession (+2.5 for Democrats), tariffs and AI job losses (see table; small fixed shifts in either direction), a running mate who carries a fraction of their own effect plus a home-state bonus, an enthusiasm channel (extra youth and Latino turnout, scaled by each candidate's mobilisation score and counted only to the extent the new voters are more Democratic than the electorate), candidate effects from the Emerson poll, correlated regional noise and state noise. Each state's shift is the Latino and non-Latino shifts weighted by that state's Latino share. Nebraska's and Maine's second districts are separate. In a share of simulations set by the compact dial, the winner is decided by the national popular vote instead of electoral votes; the electoral vote chart below still shows the underlying state result.

Limits. This is a scenario tool, not a prediction. The default setting is more favourable to Democrats than the prediction markets, where no Democrat is priced above about 22% to win the presidency and Vance and Rubio together sit near 25 to 30%. I did not find a single party-level figure, so I cannot say exactly how far apart we are. The "Markets-ish" preset (about 10 to 12% retention) gives roughly even odds and shows how pessimistic you would need to be to get near them. Redistricting changes do not affect the Electoral College, but turnout machinery, candidate nominations and a Trump third-term or health event are not modelled.
